Baseline TGLF surrogates for MAST-U and ITER, including a recreated TGLF-NN

built
Published

October 3, 2026

new component Entry 18 · milestone M3

M2 closed on 3 October with its three acceptance criteria met (M2 page); active learning moved into M3 (OQ-18). M3 trains the surrogates: neural networks that return TGLF’s turbulent fluxes from TGLF’s local inputs, fast enough to call at every radius and time step (see the Primer). The owner set one ensemble per machine, every TGLF case kept, and the fluxes learned in a signed-log space, \(\mathrm{sign}(Q)\ln(1+|Q|)\) with \(Q\) in gyro-Bohm units (gB), because the heat fluxes in the datasets span many orders of magnitude (K-019). The accuracy target was to be set after a first baseline.

Two networks

The baseline is a deep ensemble of 10 multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), each with four hidden layers of 256 units, trained on the same cases and differing in initialisation and minibatch order. The members’ mean is the prediction; their spread is the uncertainty.

The owner then asked for a recreation of TGLF-NN, the neural-network version of TGLF developed at General Atomics (GA) and distributed in TurbulentTransport.jl under Apache-2.0 (Neiser et al., APS-DPP 2022). GA’s training procedure is not published; the model files are, and we read the architecture from them. Each of 20 members is a residual network of width 32: an input layer, five blocks of four dense layers with a skip connection around each block, and a linear output layer. It takes GA’s choice of inputs, including the pressure and q gradients, and standardises its outputs linearly. We train it with our loss, optimiser and data. GA’s trained weights are not used.

Held-out errors and learning curves

Each machine’s 105 cases are split at random into training, validation and test sets (80/10/10). The test cases are used once, at the end; the error is that of the ensemble mean against TGLF, on cases with |Q| above 1 gB.

GA’s linear output scaling fails on these datasets. Standardising divides each flux by its standard deviation over the training set, and the high-flux tail sets that value: 2.5 × 105 gB (MAST-U) and 1.1 × 104 gB (ITER); the median |Q_e|, 473 and 29 gB, is 0.0019 and 0.0027 standard deviationsC-085. A squared-error loss in those units is decided by the largest cases, and the typical case is not learned. GA’s training fluxes come from measured profiles and stay in a bounded range (GA’s MAST-U model -1.5 × 104 to 5.2 × 104 gB; tglf-mastu-v1 -2.4 × 106 to 2.8 × 107 gBC-086), where the same scaling works. On signed-log targets the same network matches the MLP ensemble with about a tenth of the parameters per member (21 827 per member (TGLF-NN network) against 203 526 (MLP)C-080), and its spread covers the TGLF value more often (TGLF-NN network 0.63 (MAST-U) and 0.66 (ITER); MLP ensemble 0.54 and 0.57C-081). Neither spread is calibrated; calibration is part of M4.

Retraining on a quarter and on half of the training split gives learning curves that are still falling at 80000 cases, with slopes -0.10 (MAST-U) and -0.19 (ITER) for the TGLF-NN network; -0.07 and -0.14 for the MLPC-083. A median error of 0.30 would need, by extrapolation, about 3 × 106 cases (ITER) and about 109 (MAST-U)C-084 drawn as in the M2 datasets. Both networks give nearly the same curves, so we suspect the sampling (a uniform box, mostly far above the instability threshold) more than the network; that is not yet tested.

What checks these numbers: the test cases took no part in training or in choosing the parameters each member keeps (that used the validation set). The blog’s figure script recomputes the medians from the saved models with its own code and reproduces the stored values. The comparison with GA is not independent: the network, training code and metrics are ours, and only the architecture is GA’s.

M3 is in progress. The active-learning round that followed, and the accuracy target set afterwards (OQ-19), are in entry 19.
